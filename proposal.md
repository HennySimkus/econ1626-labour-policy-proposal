# A Workforce Transition Scheme for Autonomous AI in Financial Services

## Managing Productivity Gains and Worker Adjustment in Australian Financial Services

# Executive Summary

High autonomy AI enables financial firms to perform transaction processing, reporting, compliance and customer administration work with fewer employee hours. Once deployed and integrated, these systems can operate continuously and process large volumes of routine work at relatively low additional cost. This can generate substantial private benefits for adopting firms, including lower wage and employee related costs, faster processing and higher output.

The policy problem is that the gains and costs of caused by AI not shared fairly amongst the financial industry. Firms can keep the savings from using less human labour, while workers whose roles or hours are reduced can lose income and face the costs of disruption. Existing government support is there to help workers after disruption, but it is majorly funded by taxpayers and hence does not provide adequate funding.

This proposal recommends a two year pilot (Autonomous AI Workforce Transition Scheme) for large regulated financial firms. Firms undertaking labour substituting AI deployment would identify affected roles, consult workers, prepare transition plans and make a proportionate contribution to a dedicated transition fund. The fund would finance accredited retraining, career advice, job matching and redeployment support. 

Ultimately, the Commonwealth would provide an estimated $12 million over two years to establish and evaluate the pilot, while covered firms would fund direct worker transition support through proportionate contributions.

# 1. The Policy Problem

## 1.1 Market Failure

High autonomy financial AI can execute core business processes with limited to no human intervention. In financial services, this includes functions such as transaction processing, reconciliation, initial compliance screening, routine reporting, fraud monitoring and customer service triage (WEF 2026). When a firm uses these systems to complete work previously performed by employees, it reduces the number of paid labour hours required for that function. 

This creates a distributional problem. Firms can retain productivity gains from 24/7 processing and avoided wages, superannuation and leave. While workers whose roles are reduced, lost or changed, bear the direct costs of adjustment. The gains from labour substitution can therefore be concentrated among large firms with the capital and technical capacity to adopt AI, while affected workers and government bear much of the cost of transition through lost or altered work and publicly funded employment and training support (Filippucci et al. 2024; OECD 2024). The policy response should therefore allow financial firms to adopt productivity enhancing AI but ensuring that firms contribute to the retraining and employment support required by affected workers.

## 1.2 Why Now

According to an industry report by Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA 2025), the Financial Services sector faces high exposure to Generative AI, receiving a score of 0.72. This reflects the sector’s heavy reliance on information processing and analytical tasks that are easily augmented by AI (JSA 2025). While this exposure does not imply that 72% of financial services jobs will disappear, it highlights that the sector is at the forefront of AI driven shifts in labour demand. 

Thus, this underscores the critical need to equitably measure and distribute both the resulting productivity gains and the worker transition costs.
The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence provides a timely policy forum because its 2026 inquiry considers AI’s implications for productivity, workforce skills, job creation and displacement, job quality, financial services adoption and gaps in regulation (Parliament of Australia 2026). The Committee’s inquiry, reporting on the 30 November 2026, is therefore an appropriate forum to raise question to, and propose a workforce transition scheme for autonomous AI in financial services.

# 2. Labour Market Effects

## 2.1 Tasks, employment and job design

As shown in Figure 1, AI changes work first through task automation and augmentation. In financial services, AI adoption is actively restructuring task organisation. The Reserve Bank of Australia notes institutions deploy advanced AI across customer relations, fraud mitigation, and document processing (RBA 2024). Demonstrating employment shifts remain highly concentrated by function rather than causing broad displacement. 

RBA data reveals that while initial technology phases focused on internal redeployment, firms increasingly expect AI to reduce overall headcounts in routine back office processing, bookkeeping, and lending assessments. Conversely, the central bank highlights that roles are actively being redesigned to focus human capital on high value, non routine capabilities like exception handling, risk management, and specialized AI oversight (Fernando, McLoughlin and Ratnayake 2025).

## 2.2 Wages, skills and job quality

Figure 1 also shows that task automation can affect wages and job design unevenly. The OECD finds that workers with skills that complement AI will experience significant wage gains, with nearly half of the AI-proficient workforce expected to command earnings within the top two income deciles (OECD 2023). Consequently, workers whose tasks are reduced by automation will face lower wages or a diminished share of work. This stresses that higher productivity does not guarantee that AI's benefits are shared fairly, rather it risks widening structural income inequalities.

## 2.3 Industry structure and equity

Figure 1 highlights that large financial firms are far better positioned to scale high autonomy AI due to superior capital, data pools, cloud infrastructure, and specialised human capital. Consequently, OECD data shows AI adoption rates among large corporations are double those of smaller businesses (OECD 2024). This gap lets dominant firms lock in huge productivity and labour cost savings, ultimately worsening competitive imbalances and market concentration within the financial ecosystem. Dominant firms should be required to share the economic gains of AI driven automation.

# 3. Policy Options

The following options are assessed against six criteria as presented in figure 2:

## Option 1: Expand Existing Worker Transition Support

DEWR could expand career advice, accredited digital and AI skills training, job matching and transition assistance for financial service workers affected by AI. This could build on Career Transition Assistance, which helps eligible people aged 45 years and over improve their skills and competitiveness in local labour markets (DEWR 2025).

This option is quick and feasible because it uses existing programs and creates no new obligations for firms. However, government and taxpayers would fund most worker support, even where firms retain labour cost savings from AI. It also does not require firms to identify affected roles or plan for workforce change before deployment.

## Option 2: Require Workforce Transition Plans

The committee proposes that firms should prepare a workforce transition plan before deploying high autonomy AI that reduces employee hours. Plans would identify affected tasks and roles, estimate changes to staffing and skill needs, consult workers, and set out redeployment or training arrangements.

This option improves transparency and encourages firms to anticipate workforce effects. It has a low public cost because firms would prepare the plans. However, plans alone do not provide funding for affected workers. Firms could identify likely job reductions without contributing to retraining or redeployment costs.

## Option 3: Autonomous AI Workforce Transition Scheme

The Committee should recommend a two year Autonomous AI Workforce Transition Scheme for financial firms that use AI to replace human labour.  Firms would report the deployment, identify affected tasks, roles and employee hours, consult workers, prepare a transition plan and contribute to a dedicated transition fund. Requirements and contributions would be proportionate to the scale of labour substitution, so small firms and minor changes face lower obligations than large scale displacement. DEWR would administer the fund for accredited retraining, career advice, job matching and redeployment. ASIC would monitor reporting and compliance under new regulatory requirements.

# 4. Options Evaluation

As per figure 3, option 1 is the easiest to implement and most compatible with AI adoption, but it leaves taxpayers to fund worker adjustment while firms retain labour cost savings. Option 2 improves transparency, consultation and early workforce planning, but it provides no reliable funding for retraining or redeployment. Option 3 performs best overall because it combines planning with a dedicated transition fund and allocates costs more fairly to firms that replace human labour with AI. Its higher administrative burden is justified by the proportionate obligations, with the extent of contribution dependent on the amount of labour substitution. A two year pilot would test definitions, costs and worker outcomes before broader implementation.

# 5. Recommended Package and Implementation

The Committee should recommend a two year Autonomous AI Workforce Transition Scheme pilot. The Australian Government would establish the scheme: DEWR would administer the transition fund and worker services, while ASIC would monitor firm reporting and compliance. As outlined in Figure 4, Treasury, DEWR, ASIC and Jobs and Skills Australia would develop materiality thresholds, reporting templates, consultation standards and a proportionate contribution formula in the first six months. In year one, participating financial services firms would lodge workforce transition plans and contributions through a central portal. The funding and accountability arrangements are set out in Figure 5: a $12 million Commonwealth appropriation would fund policy design, guidance, administration and independent evaluation, while firm contributions would primarily fund direct worker support. Figure 6 sets out KPIs for the year two evaluation before a continuation decision.

# 6. Risks and Mitigations

As set out in Figure 7, the scheme creates risks of deterring productive AI adoption, regulatory avoidance, superficial transition planning and ineffective training. These risks are managed through a targeted, proportionate pilot: obligations scale with material labour substitution, reporting is auditable, worker consultation is evidenced, and success is measured by employment and earnings outcomes rather than course enrolments alone. The independent evaluation would review contribution settings, compliance costs and investment effects before any continuation or expansion decision.
